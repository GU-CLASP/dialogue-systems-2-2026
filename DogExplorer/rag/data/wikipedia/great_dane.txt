Overview:
The Great Dane is a large German dog breed of the mastiff-sighthound types which is descended from hunting dogs of the Middle Ages that were used to hunt bear, boar and deer. They were also used as guard dogs of German nobility. Along with the Irish Wolfhound, it is among the tallest dog breeds in the world.


Description:
The Great Dane is an extremely large domestic dog of mastiff-sighthound type known for its big size. It is often called the "Apollo of dogs".
As described by the American Kennel Club:
The Great Dane combines, in its regal appearance, strength, and elegance with great size and a powerful, well-formed, smoothly muscled body. It is one of the giant working breeds, but is unique in that its general conformation must be so well balanced that it never appears clumsy, and shall move with a long reach and powerful drive. The Great Dane is a short-haired breed with a strong, galloping figure. In the ratio between length and height, the Great Dane should be square. The male dog should not be less than 76 cm (30 in) at the shoulders, a female 71 cm (28 in). Danes under minimum height are disqualified. Over the years, the tallest living dog has typically been a Great Dane. Previous record holders include Gibson, Titan, and Giant George; however, the current record holder is a black Great Dane named Zeus that stood 111.8 cm (44 in) at the shoulder before his death in September 2014. He was also the tallest dog on record (according to the Guinness World Records), beating the previous record holder, the aforementioned Giant George that stood 109.2 cm (43 in) at the shoulder.

Coat:
According to the breed standard, Great Danes have seven to eight (depending on the standard) show-acceptable coat colours:
Fawn and brindle
Fawn: The colour is yellow-gold with a black mask. Black should appear on the eye rims and eyebrows and may appear on the ears.
Brindle: The colour is fawn and black in a chevron striped pattern. Often they are referred to as having a striped pattern.
Black, harlequin, grey merle and mantle
Black: The colour is a glossy black. White markings on the chest and toes are not desirable and considered faults.
Harlequin: The base colour is pure white with torn black patches irregularly distributed over the entire body; a pure white neck is preferred. The black patches should never be large enough to give the appearance of a blanket, nor so small as to give a stippled or dappled effect. Eligible, but less desirable, are a few small patches of grey that is consistent with a merle marking, or a white base with single black hairs showing through, which tend to give a salt and pepper or dirty effect. Merlequin, a white coat with grey patches instead of black, is a disqualification.
Grey merle (Grautiger) Great Danes are acceptable in conformation shows under the FCI. This colour was previously a disqualifying fault, but the fault was deleted in a new breed standard in 2012 to provide a wider gene pool and because the grey merle gene can produce a correct harlequin coat. Health:
Like most giant dogs, Great Danes have a fairly slow metabolism. This results in less energy and less food consumption per pound of dog than in small breeds. A neutered adult of about 140 lb (64 kg) needs roughly 2,200–2,500 kcal per day. They have some health problems that are common to large breeds, which includes bloat (gastric dilatation volvulus (GDV)).
Nutrition plays a role in this breed's health. Bloat, or gastric dilatation-volvulus (GDV), is the greatest killer of Great Danes. To avoid bloat, a rest period of 40 minutes to one hour after meals is recommended before exercise.
One study of large and giant-breed dogs attributed about 52% of GDV cases in giant breeds to the use of a raised feeding bowl. A model of Great Danes estimated a lifetime GDV risk of about 37%. The same model found that a preventive gastropexy could greatly reduce deaths from the condition.
A 2024 UK study found the average life expectancy of the breed to be 10.6 years, compared to an average of 12.7 for purebreeds and 12 for crossbreeds. A 2005 Swedish study of insurance records found 83% of Great Danes died by the age of 10, higher than the overall rate of 35% of dogs dying by the age of 10.
Dilated cardiomyopathy and many congenital heart diseases are also commonly found in the Great Dane, leading to its nickname: the heartbreak breed, in conjunction with its shorter lifespan.
